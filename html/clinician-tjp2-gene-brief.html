High-level Gene Facts

TJP2 is the official gene symbol for tight junction protein 2, also called ZO-2 or zonula occludens 2. NCBI Gene lists it as Gene ID 9414, a protein-coding gene at 9q21.11. The encoded protein is a component of epithelial and endothelial tight-junction barriers.

PFIC4 is generally used for progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis caused by biallelic pathogenic TJP2 variants. OMIM links TJP2 with autosomal recessive PFIC4 and familial hypercholanemia; NCBI also lists a separate association between genomic duplication including TJP2 and autosomal dominant deafness-51.

Why This Gene Causes Cholestasis

TJP2/ZO-2 is a scaffolding protein at cell-cell junctions. In hepatocytes and cholangiocytes, that junctional architecture helps keep bile canalicular contents within the canalicular space.

  • TJP2 deficiency or dysfunction disrupts tight-junction organization.
  • Claudin-1 localization at cholangiocyte borders and bile-canalicular margins can fail even when total claudin-1 protein is present.
  • Early reports showed elongated tight junctions and loss of the densest zona occludens component.
  • Bile acids and other bile constituents can reflux or leak through paracellular spaces into liver parenchyma.
  • Chronic exposure to detergent bile acids contributes to cholestasis, pruritus, hepatocyte injury, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and cancer concern.
PFIC4 is a barrier and polarity problem, not primarily a transporter-pump defect like BSEP deficiency. IBAT inhibition or biliary diversion may still help by lowering enterohepatic bile-acid burden.

Variant Interpretation And Phenotype Range

The first PFIC4 families reported by Sambrotta et al. had variants predicted to abolish TJP2 translation, absent hepatic TJP2 expression, disrupted claudin-1 localization, and severe pediatric cholestatic liver disease. Later case reports and series expanded the phenotype to include missense, splice, and other variants with variable severity.

  • Confirm exact transcript, cDNA/protein notation, zygosity, inheritance phase, and classification.
  • Distinguish predicted loss-of-function variants from missense or splice variants that may allow residual protein function.
  • Treat genotype-phenotype prediction as useful but incomplete.
  • A low or normal GGT cholestasis pattern is common in reported PFIC4, but PFIC4 cannot be diagnosed or excluded by GGT alone.
  • Check whether deletion/duplication analysis was included.

Clinical Implications

Reported PFIC4 presentations include neonatal or childhood cholestasis, jaundice, severe pruritus, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency, growth failure, portal hypertension, cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Recurrent or later-onset disease is also described.

HCC deserves specific attention. Published TJP2-deficiency cases include childhood HCC and adult families with cirrhosis/HCC. The evidence base is small, but the signal is strong enough to justify explicit surveillance discussion with a pediatric genetic cholestasis or transplant hepatology team.

Diagnostic And Monitoring Considerations

  • Cholestasis labs: total/direct bilirubin, AST, ALT, GGT, alkaline phosphatase, serum bile acids, INR, albumin, CBC/platelets.
  • Imaging to exclude extrahepatic obstruction or biliary atresia when clinically relevant.
  • Genetic testing with TJP2 sequencing plus deletion/duplication analysis if not already included.
  • Family segregation testing when needed to prove biallelic disease.
  • Histology or immunostaining for TJP2 and claudin-1 only if tissue is already clinically indicated.

Treatment Relevance

The gene-level defect is not currently corrected by approved PFIC medications. Current treatment logic is indirect: IBAT inhibitors and biliary diversion reduce bile-acid return to the liver; nutrition, vitamin monitoring, antipruritic adjuncts, fibrosis/portal-hypertension surveillance, and HCC surveillance remain central; liver transplant remains definitive for end-stage disease, HCC, or refractory symptoms/complications.
