# Holistic options and Livmarli alternatives

Reviewed: 2026-11-24. Scope: PFIC4 / TJP2-associated cholestasis.

HTML companion: [Read the page options](../html/holistic-and-livmarli-alternatives-draft.html).

Three draft layouts for review: A compares options, B explains mechanisms, and C prepares for an appointment. The shared research reviews and sources follow. Choose a layout before integrating this page into the packet.

No holistic replacement with clinical evidence comparable to Livmarli for PFIC4 was identified in the sources reviewed. Plausible mechanisms are research leads, not established treatments. This research is for discussion with a hepatology team; do not use it to stop, replace, or combine prescribed medicines independently.

## A. Compare the options

### What might replace or complement Livmarli?

**Livmarli is maralixibat; Bylvay is odevixibat.** These are two medicines, each with a brand name and an active-ingredient name. Both reversibly inhibit the same intestinal transporter, IBAT / ASBT. Sharing a target does not make their dosing, safety, or clinical response interchangeable. [1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=64000394-1ef6-4e76-8ba8-11f25ba1b167), [2](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=151f1d3e-2bf4-47ce-b1be-a892de3258fa)

The similarity column separates **how an option works** from **whether comparable effects have been demonstrated in patients**. Laboratory overlap is not a measure of treatment strength or a reason to substitute a supplement.

The closest established lower-cost strategy to discuss is a prescription bile-acid binder. That is a mechanistic comparison, not a claim of equal benefit. Livmarli's label requires at least four hours of separation before or after bile-acid binding resins. A pharmacist should also review other medicines and vitamin timing. [1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=64000394-1ef6-4e76-8ba8-11f25ba1b167)

## B. Follow the mechanism

### 1. Reduce bile-acid recycling

Livmarli and Bylvay block a transporter in the last part of the small intestine. Binders capture bile acids already in the gut. Both strategies aim to increase loss in stool, but they have different evidence and practical limits. Livmarli is minimally absorbed; it mainly acts in the gut, although it still has important risks and monitoring requirements. [1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=64000394-1ef6-4e76-8ba8-11f25ba1b167), [2](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=151f1d3e-2bf4-47ce-b1be-a892de3258fa), [3](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=362ddd91-a63f-4ec6-841a-75785dd208c8)

### 2. Examine natural compounds that affect the same transporter

**Resveratrol: cell-study evidence.** Experiments found reduced ASBT activity and increased transporter breakdown. This is a plausible connection to Livmarli's target, not proof of a safe or effective PFIC4 treatment. No human PFIC4 replacement trial was identified. [6](https://doi.org/10.1042/BJ20131428)

**Green-tea EGCG: cell-study evidence with a liver safety concern.** EGCG inhibited ASBT in cultured cells. Concentrated green-tea extracts have also caused liver injury. This is not a recommended supplement experiment for a child with liver disease. [7](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/ajpgi.00360.2009), [8](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/green-tea)

[Read the deeper evidence review](#resveratrol-and-egcg-evidence-review) for the contradictory bile-acid results and the limits of pediatric safety findings.

### 3. Separate cell protection from bile-acid removal

**TUDCA: related-disease evidence and mechanistic inference.** TUDCA is taurine-conjugated ursodiol, not an herbal IBAT inhibitor. PFIC4 benefit is unestablished. A neonatal trial in parenteral-nutrition-associated cholestasis found no benefit; that different disease cannot settle PFIC4 efficacy either way. [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12183720/)

**Milk thistle: uncertain human liver-disease evidence.** Its main extract, silymarin, has proposed antioxidant effects, but human liver-disease studies have been inconsistent or insufficient. It has not been shown to reproduce Livmarli's PFIC4 benefit. [10](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/milk-thistle)

## C. Prepare for the appointment

### A focused discussion about cost and treatment

1. **Define what is working.** Review itching, sleep, serum bile acids, bilirubin, liver tests, growth, vitamin levels, and the existing fibrosis and surveillance plan. Symptom relief and disease monitoring are separate goals.
2. **Review previous treatment precisely.** Identify whether earlier "bile salts" meant ursodiol or a binder. Record benefit, side effects, and why each medicine stopped.
3. **Ask whether a binder is realistic.** Discuss expected benefit, formulation, constipation, vitamin absorption, and medicine timing. Set response targets and stopping rules before any clinician-directed change.
4. **Review supplement safety.** Bring the actual bottle, serving size, lot number, and full medicine list. Review Dose's enhanced-absorption curcumin specifically.
5. **Compare access options.** Separate the billed price from family out-of-pocket cost. Mirum Access Plus provides coverage navigation and financial-assistance screening. Eligibility varies; Bylvay should not be assumed cheaper without a coverage review. [14](https://livmarli.com/progressive-familial-intrahepatic-cholestasis/access-and-support)

This discussion framework draws on the labels and pediatric cholestatic-pruritus review; it is not a medication-switch protocol. [1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=64000394-1ef6-4e76-8ba8-11f25ba1b167), [3](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=362ddd91-a63f-4ec6-841a-75785dd208c8), [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10215450/)

## Resveratrol and EGCG evidence review

Neither compound has demonstrated reliable serum bile-acid lowering or an established safe treatment regimen in children with PFIC4 in the clinical evidence identified. **Resveratrol remains a research lead. Concentrated EGCG has additional human liver-safety concerns.** A shared transporter target in cells does not establish a useful or safe substitute for Livmarli or Bylvay.

### Why the bile-acid results conflict

Three different measurements must be kept separate: transporter activity in cells, bile acids leaving in stool, and bile acids circulating in blood or accumulating in the liver. The total bile-acid pool is another measurement. More loss in stool can be offset by changes in synthesis and transport; it does not automatically mean lower blood levels. The mouse resveratrol experiment below demonstrates this distinction. [16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4817264/)

| Compound and study | What actually happened | What this means for PFIC4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resveratrol: cell experiments | ASBT activity and protein expression fell through increased transporter breakdown. [6](https://doi.org/10.1042/BJ20131428) | Preclinical target overlap, without proof of human intestinal target engagement, serum bile-acid reduction, or clinical benefit. |
| Resveratrol: mouse bile-acid metabolism study | Fecal bile-acid loss increased, but serum and liver bile acids did not significantly change. Bile-acid synthesis and the measured total pool increased. ASBT expression did not significantly change in this experiment. [16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4817264/) | Direct counterexample to assuming that increased fecal loss or a cell result guarantees lower blood bile acids. This was not a TJP2-deficiency model. |
| Resveratrol: chemically induced cholestasis in mice | Pretreatment reduced the chemical-induced elevation in serum total bile acids in mice with functioning FXR signaling; the effect was absent in FXR-knockout mice. [17](https://www.spandidos-publications.com/10.3892/mmr.2017.8051) | A positive preclinical result, but prevention of acute chemical injury does not establish treatment of chronic genetic barrier failure. |
| EGCG: cell experiments | ASBT activity decreased in cultured kidney and intestinal cell models. [7](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/ajpgi.00360.2009) | Shows a possible mechanism, not the net effect of an oral supplement in a patient. |
| EGCG: high-fat-diet mouse study | Intestinal bile-acid content decreased and fecal bile-acid excretion increased. [18](https://doi.org/10.1002/mnfr.201700696) | Supports altered intestinal handling in a metabolic-disease model; does not establish reduced blood bile acids in PFIC4. |
| EGCG: rat biliary-physiology study | Blood bile acids doubled and bile flow decreased by 23%, despite reduced intestinal Asbt expression. Changes included increased bile-acid synthesis and reduced Mrp2-associated bile flow. [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23146761/) | A contradictory preclinical result with potential harm. The authors concluded that EGCG could induce cholestasis under some conditions. It does not predict an individual child's response. |

The studies differ in species, disease model, formulation, exposure, and endpoints. They cannot be combined into a percentage of Livmarli-like activity or converted into a pediatric dose. No clinical PFIC4 trial establishing benefit for either compound was identified.

### What the safety evidence does and does not establish

| Study or assessment | Safety findings | Why it does not establish pediatric PFIC4 safety |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resveratrol: NIH LiverTox, updated November 30, 2024 | No convincing link to clinically apparent liver injury in that review; some high-dose adult studies reported liver-enzyme increases. [20](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548465/) | A relatively reassuring general liver-injury record is not a safety study in children with TJP2-associated cholestasis. |
| Resveratrol: open-label muscular-dystrophy study | Eleven participants aged 12 to 46 received treatment for 24 weeks. Six experienced diarrhea and three abdominal pain. Liver and kidney blood markers were not affected in this small study. [21](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-77197-6) | Mixed adolescents and adults, another disease, no placebo group, and too few participants to establish uncommon risks. It provides no PFIC4-specific dosing or safety evidence. |
| Green-tea extract: randomized adult safety analysis | Among 513 treated adults in the analysis, 26, or 5.1%, developed moderate or worse liver-test abnormalities. Risk was higher than placebo; some abnormalities resolved after withdrawal and recurred after restarting. The extract supplied approximately 843 mg EGCG daily. [22](https://aacrjournals.org/cancerpreventionresearch/article/10/10/571/112911/Effect-of-Green-Tea-Supplements-on-Liver-Enzyme) | A human liver-injury signal, not a pediatric risk estimate. The adult study exposure is not a dose recommendation or a safe-dose threshold. |
| EGCG: PERSEUS pediatric trial | Seventy-three children aged 6 to 12 with Down syndrome were randomized; 72 received treatment. No liver-enzyme alterations were reported during the monitored study. [23](https://www.gimjournal.org/article/S1098-3600(22)00820-6/fulltext) | The trial excluded previous liver disease, significant hepatic laboratory abnormalities, and clinically significant unstable hepatic disease. Children with those features were not represented by its reassuring result. [24](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03624556) |

**The pediatric exclusion is decisive for interpreting applicability.** A study designed to avoid children with liver disease cannot establish safety in children whose underlying condition is liver disease. PERSEUS studied another diagnosis, a specific formulation, and six months of monitored treatment. Its small size also cannot exclude uncommon or longer-term harms. These are limits on applying the findings, not evidence that every exposed child would be harmed. [23](https://www.gimjournal.org/article/S1098-3600(22)00820-6/fulltext), [24](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03624556)

The U.S. Pharmacopeia review describes a green-tea-extract warning: **"Do not use if you have a liver problem."** It also notes that fasting can increase EGCG exposure. Taking an extract with food or choosing a lower dose does not establish PFIC4 safety. This review concerns concentrated extracts; ordinary brewed tea is a different exposure and is not an established PFIC4 treatment either. [25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7044683/), [8](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/green-tea)

### Implications for treatment discussions

Resveratrol is worth discussing as a research question, with mixed preclinical results and unproven PFIC4 benefit. Concentrated EGCG has an unfavorable basis for a pediatric PFIC4 supplement trial outside appropriate clinical research, given unproven benefit and the human liver-injury signal. Neither evidence base supports independently adding these supplements or reducing an effective prescribed IBAT inhibitor.

There is no established safe or effective pediatric PFIC4 regimen for either compound in the evidence reviewed. A credible future study would need to measure serum bile acids and liver safety alongside itch, sleep, growth, nutrition, and longer-term disease outcomes, rather than relying on stool excretion or a single liver-enzyme change.

## Dose for your Liver

### Ingredients and what the label leaves unknown

The NIH label archive, entered March 25, 2025, lists a **435 mg proprietary blend per 2-ounce serving**. Individual ingredient amounts are not disclosed. The current bottle should be checked for formulation changes. An NIH label listing is not an endorsement or evidence of pediatric safety. [11](https://dsld.od.nih.gov/label/328203)

| Botanical ingredient | Relevant constituent or role | What this establishes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organic turmeric extract | Curcumin-containing extract | An ingredient in the blend, not evidence of PFIC4 treatment. |
| Organic milk thistle extract | Silymarin-containing extract | General liver-support rationale; clinical evidence remains uncertain. |
| Organic dandelion powder | Botanical powder | No PFIC4 benefit established by this label. |
| Organic ginger powder | Botanical powder | No PFIC4 benefit established by this label. |

Other listed ingredients are purified water, erythritol, orange juice concentrate, citric acid, potassium citrate, sodium citrate, flavors, xanthan gum, and monk fruit extract. They form the drink's liquid base and help with taste, acidity, or texture; the label does not identify them as PFIC4 treatments. [11](https://dsld.od.nih.gov/label/328203)

### What the clinical study actually tested

A manufacturer-funded randomized study enrolled **130 healthy adults aged 18 to 60** for six months and excluded people with liver disease. It reported changes in liver enzymes. It does not establish pediatric PFIC4 benefit, bile-acid reduction, itch control, or prevention of liver damage. Lower ALT alone would not demonstrate equivalent disease control. [12](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2025.1721748/full)

### The curcumin safety question

Dose advertises enhanced curcumin absorption. NIH warns that some enhanced-bioavailability curcumin supplements have caused liver injury. This does not establish that Dose itself causes injury, but it warrants hepatology review before use, continuation, or an increase in a child with liver disease. Concentrated supplements differ from ordinary turmeric used in food. [13](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/turmeric), [15](https://dosedaily.co/products/dose-for-your-liver-new)
