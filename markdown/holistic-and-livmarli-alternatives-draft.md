# Holistic options and Livmarli alternatives

Reviewed: 2026-11-24. Scope: PFIC4 / TJP2-associated cholestasis.

HTML companion: [Read the page options](../html/holistic-and-livmarli-alternatives-draft.html).

Three draft layouts for review: A compares options, B explains mechanisms, and C prepares for an appointment. The shared Dose review and sources follow. Choose a layout before integrating this page into the packet.

No holistic replacement with clinical evidence comparable to Livmarli for PFIC4 was identified in the sources reviewed. Plausible mechanisms are research leads, not established treatments. This research is for discussion with a hepatology team; do not use it to stop, replace, or combine prescribed medicines independently.

## A. Compare the options

### What might replace or complement Livmarli?

| Option | What it does | Evidence relevant to PFIC4 | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Livmarli / maralixibat | Blocks intestinal bile-acid reabsorption through IBAT, also called ASBT. | Direct PFIC4 inclusion: seven PFIC4 patients in the randomized trial's efficacy population. | Too few for firm subtype-specific conclusions; does not repair TJP2. [1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=64000394-1ef6-4e76-8ba8-11f25ba1b167) |
| Bylvay / odevixibat | Targets the same bile-acid recycling transporter. | PFIC-labeled; pivotal randomized trial studied PFIC1/PFIC2, with broader open-label evidence. | PFIC4-specific evidence is more limited. Compare actual coverage and cost before considering a switch. [2](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=151f1d3e-2bf4-47ce-b1be-a892de3258fa) |
| Cholestyramine; other prescription binders include colesevelam and colestipol | Bind bile acids inside the gut, interrupting recycling through a different mechanism. | Broader cholestatic-pruritus evidence; not established PFIC4 equivalents. | Constipation, medicine binding, and vitamin malabsorption. Colesevelam lowered bile acids without beating placebo for itch in an adult trial. [1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=64000394-1ef6-4e76-8ba8-11f25ba1b167), [3](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=362ddd91-a63f-4ec6-841a-75785dd208c8), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20683930/) |
| Ursodiol / UDCA | Changes bile-acid composition and supports bile flow. | Broader pediatric cholestasis evidence; established PFIC4 disease modification is lacking. | Different treatment goal from IBAT inhibition; response varies. [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10215450/) |
| Rifampin | Acts on pathways involved in cholestatic itch. | Broader pediatric cholestatic-pruritus evidence. | Not an IBAT substitute; liver injury and interactions require monitoring. [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10215450/) |
| Dose and other herbal liver supplements | Marketed for liver support, usually through botanical mixtures. | Adult or laboratory evidence, depending on the product; no comparable clinical PFIC4 replacement evidence identified. | Ingredient-specific liver risks, uncertain pediatric dosing, and no established replacement benefit. See the Dose review below. |

The closest established lower-cost strategy to discuss is a prescription bile-acid binder. That is a mechanistic comparison, not a claim of equal benefit. Livmarli's label requires at least four hours of separation before or after bile-acid binding resins. A pharmacist should also review other medicines and vitamin timing. [1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=64000394-1ef6-4e76-8ba8-11f25ba1b167)

## B. Follow the mechanism

### 1. Reduce bile-acid recycling

Livmarli and Bylvay block a transporter in the last part of the small intestine. Binders capture bile acids already in the gut. Both strategies aim to increase loss in stool, but they have different evidence and practical limits. Livmarli is minimally absorbed; it mainly acts in the gut, although it still has important risks and monitoring requirements. [1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=64000394-1ef6-4e76-8ba8-11f25ba1b167), [2](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=151f1d3e-2bf4-47ce-b1be-a892de3258fa), [3](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=362ddd91-a63f-4ec6-841a-75785dd208c8)

### 2. Examine natural compounds that affect the same transporter

**Resveratrol: cell-study evidence.** Experiments found reduced ASBT activity and increased transporter breakdown. This is a plausible connection to Livmarli's target, not proof of a safe or effective PFIC4 treatment. No human PFIC4 replacement trial was identified. [6](https://doi.org/10.1042/BJ20131428)

**Green-tea EGCG: cell-study evidence with a liver safety concern.** EGCG inhibited ASBT in cultured cells. Concentrated green-tea extracts have also caused liver injury. This is not a recommended supplement experiment for a child with liver disease. [7](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/ajpgi.00360.2009), [8](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/green-tea)

### 3. Separate cell protection from bile-acid removal

**TUDCA: related-disease evidence and mechanistic inference.** TUDCA is taurine-conjugated ursodiol, not an herbal IBAT inhibitor. PFIC4 benefit is unestablished. A neonatal trial in parenteral-nutrition-associated cholestasis found no benefit; that different disease cannot settle PFIC4 efficacy either way. [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12183720/)

**Milk thistle: uncertain human liver-disease evidence.** Its main extract, silymarin, has proposed antioxidant effects, but human liver-disease studies have been inconsistent or insufficient. It has not been shown to reproduce Livmarli's PFIC4 benefit. [10](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/milk-thistle)

## C. Prepare for the appointment

### A focused discussion about cost and treatment

1. **Define what is working.** Review itching, sleep, serum bile acids, bilirubin, liver tests, growth, vitamin levels, and the existing fibrosis and surveillance plan. Symptom relief and disease monitoring are separate goals.
2. **Review previous treatment precisely.** Identify whether earlier "bile salts" meant ursodiol or a binder. Record benefit, side effects, and why each medicine stopped.
3. **Ask whether a binder is realistic.** Discuss expected benefit, formulation, constipation, vitamin absorption, and medicine timing. Set response targets and stopping rules before any clinician-directed change.
4. **Review supplement safety.** Bring the actual bottle, serving size, lot number, and full medicine list. Review Dose's enhanced-absorption curcumin specifically.
5. **Compare access options.** Separate the billed price from family out-of-pocket cost. Mirum Access Plus provides coverage navigation and financial-assistance screening. Eligibility varies; Bylvay should not be assumed cheaper without a coverage review. [14](https://livmarli.com/progressive-familial-intrahepatic-cholestasis/access-and-support)

This discussion framework draws on the labels and pediatric cholestatic-pruritus review; it is not a medication-switch protocol. [1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=64000394-1ef6-4e76-8ba8-11f25ba1b167), [3](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=362ddd91-a63f-4ec6-841a-75785dd208c8), [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10215450/)

## Dose for your Liver

### Ingredients and what the label leaves unknown

The NIH label archive, entered March 25, 2025, lists a **435 mg proprietary blend per 2-ounce serving**. Individual ingredient amounts are not disclosed. The current bottle should be checked for formulation changes. An NIH label listing is not an endorsement or evidence of pediatric safety. [11](https://dsld.od.nih.gov/label/328203)

| Botanical ingredient | Relevant constituent or role | What this establishes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organic turmeric extract | Curcumin-containing extract | An ingredient in the blend, not evidence of PFIC4 treatment. |
| Organic milk thistle extract | Silymarin-containing extract | General liver-support rationale; clinical evidence remains uncertain. |
| Organic dandelion powder | Botanical powder | No PFIC4 benefit established by this label. |
| Organic ginger powder | Botanical powder | No PFIC4 benefit established by this label. |

Other listed ingredients are purified water, erythritol, orange juice concentrate, citric acid, potassium citrate, sodium citrate, flavors, xanthan gum, and monk fruit extract. They form the drink's liquid base and help with taste, acidity, or texture; the label does not identify them as PFIC4 treatments. [11](https://dsld.od.nih.gov/label/328203)

### What the clinical study actually tested

A manufacturer-funded randomized study enrolled **130 healthy adults aged 18 to 60** for six months and excluded people with liver disease. It reported changes in liver enzymes. It does not establish pediatric PFIC4 benefit, bile-acid reduction, itch control, or prevention of liver damage. Lower ALT alone would not demonstrate equivalent disease control. [12](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2025.1721748/full)

### The curcumin safety question

Dose advertises enhanced curcumin absorption. NIH warns that some enhanced-bioavailability curcumin supplements have caused liver injury. This does not establish that Dose itself causes injury, but it warrants hepatology review before use, continuation, or an increase in a child with liver disease. Concentrated supplements differ from ordinary turmeric used in food. [13](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/turmeric), [15](https://dosedaily.co/products/dose-for-your-liver-new)
