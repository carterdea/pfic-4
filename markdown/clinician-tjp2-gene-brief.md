## High-level gene facts

`TJP2` is the official gene symbol for tight junction protein 2, also called ZO-2 or zonula occludens 2. NCBI Gene lists it as Gene ID 9414, a protein-coding gene at 9q21.11. The encoded protein is a member of the membrane-associated guanylate kinase homolog family and is a component of epithelial and endothelial tight-junction barriers.

PFIC4 is generally used for progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis caused by biallelic pathogenic `TJP2` variants. OMIM links `TJP2` with autosomal recessive PFIC4 and familial hypercholanemia; NCBI also lists a separate association between genomic duplication including `TJP2` and autosomal dominant deafness-51. Those non-PFIC associations matter for variant interpretation but should not be overread as explaining liver phenotype without genotype context.

## Why this gene causes cholestasis

TJP2/ZO-2 is a scaffolding protein at cell-cell junctions. In hepatocytes and cholangiocytes, that junctional architecture helps keep bile canalicular contents within the canalicular space. The recurring PFIC4 model is:

- TJP2 deficiency or dysfunction disrupts tight-junction organization.
- Claudin-1 localization at cholangiocyte borders and bile-canalicular margins can fail even when total claudin-1 protein is present.
- Electron microscopy in early reports showed elongated tight junctions and loss of the densest zona occludens component.
- Bile acids and other bile constituents can reflux or leak through paracellular spaces into liver parenchyma.
- Chronic exposure to detergent bile acids contributes to cholestasis, pruritus, hepatocyte injury, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and cancer concern.

This is a barrier and polarity problem, not primarily a transporter-pump defect like BSEP deficiency. It still provides a rationale for enterohepatic-circulation interruption: if bile acids can be secreted and reach the intestine, IBAT inhibition or biliary diversion may lower the bile-acid pool even though neither strategy corrects the tight-junction defect.

## Variant interpretation and phenotype range

The first PFIC4 families reported by Sambrotta et al. had variants predicted to abolish TJP2 translation, absent hepatic TJP2 expression, disrupted claudin-1 localization, and severe pediatric cholestatic liver disease. Later case reports and series expanded the phenotype to include missense, splice, and other variants with variable severity.

Practical points for clinician review:

- Confirm exact transcript, cDNA/protein notation, zygosity, inheritance phase, and classification.
- Distinguish predicted loss-of-function variants from missense or splice variants that may allow residual protein function.
- Treat genotype-phenotype prediction as useful but incomplete. Published reports describe pronounced variability, including within related families or apparently similar molecular classes.
- A low or normal GGT cholestasis pattern is common in reported PFIC4, but PFIC4 cannot be diagnosed or excluded by GGT alone.
- Consider whether genetic testing was a targeted cholestasis panel, exome, genome, or reanalysis, and whether copy-number analysis was included.

## Clinical implications

Reported PFIC4 presentations include neonatal or childhood cholestasis, jaundice, severe pruritus, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency, growth failure, portal hypertension, cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Recurrent or later-onset disease is also described, so severity at one time point should not be assumed to define lifetime risk.

Extrahepatic findings have been reported in some patients, including respiratory, neurologic, hematologic/bleeding, and hearing-related findings. Because TJP2 is broadly expressed, these should be reviewed clinically, but liver disease remains the dominant PFIC4 management issue in the cited literature.

HCC deserves specific attention. Published TJP2-deficiency cases include childhood HCC and adult families with cirrhosis/HCC. The evidence base is small, but the signal is strong enough to justify explicit surveillance discussion with a pediatric genetic cholestasis or transplant hepatology team.

## Diagnostic and monitoring considerations

Potential diagnostic workup elements to confirm or contextualize PFIC4:

- Cholestasis labs including total/direct bilirubin, AST, ALT, GGT, alkaline phosphatase, serum bile acids, INR, albumin, CBC/platelets.
- Imaging to exclude extrahepatic obstruction or biliary atresia when clinically relevant.
- Genetic testing with `TJP2` sequencing plus deletion/duplication analysis if not already included.
- Family segregation testing when needed to prove biallelic disease.
- Histology or immunostaining for TJP2 and claudin-1 only if tissue is already clinically indicated; genetic testing is usually the cleaner diagnostic route.

Monitoring questions to clarify:

- What HCC surveillance interval does the center use for PFIC4 specifically?
- Does surveillance start at diagnosis, at fibrosis/cirrhosis, or after another risk threshold?
- What response thresholds define meaningful improvement in serum bile acids, pruritus, bilirubin, growth, and sleep?
- What objective findings trigger discussion of biliary diversion, transplant evaluation, or cancer workup?
- Are there extrahepatic screening needs based on the exact variants or patient phenotype?

## Treatment relevance

The gene-level defect is not currently corrected by approved PFIC medications. Current treatment logic is indirect:

- IBAT inhibitors reduce intestinal bile-acid reabsorption and lower enterohepatic bile-acid recirculation.
- Biliary diversion similarly aims to reduce bile-acid return to the liver in selected patients before advanced cirrhosis.
- Nutrition, fat-soluble vitamin monitoring, antipruritic adjuncts, fibrosis/portal-hypertension surveillance, and HCC surveillance remain central.
- Liver transplant remains the definitive option for end-stage disease, HCC, or refractory symptoms/complications when other approaches fail.
