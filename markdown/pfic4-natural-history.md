# PFIC4 natural history

This is a research summary about PFIC4 natural history for discussion with a hepatology, genetics, transplant, or rare-disease team. It is not medical advice and does not predict any one patient's course.

## Bottom line

- PFIC4 natural history is variable. Published `TJP2` disease ranges from early severe infantile cholestasis to milder, intermittent, later-onset, pregnancy-triggered, or adult-recognized disease.
- The strongest genotype signal is that biallelic predicted protein-truncating variants, especially when both alleles are predicted truncating, are associated with earlier and more severe disease.
- Missense and some splice or in-frame variants may leave residual TJP2 function and can be milder, but missense disease is not automatically benign.
- Genotype does not fully predict phenotype. Same-family and same-variant reports show different ages of onset, different severity, and possible modifier effects from other cholestasis genes or triggers.
- HCC concern is real but based on small numbers. It should trigger an explicit surveillance plan rather than a false certainty statement.

## What is known about early severe disease

The first PFIC4 report, Sambrotta et al. 2014, described 12 affected children from eight families with chronic low-GGT cholestatic liver disease. All presented in the first three months of life. The reported variants were predicted to abolish TJP2 translation; hepatic studies showed absent TJP2 protein, abnormal claudin-1 localization, and tight-junction structural disruption. Nine of 12 required liver transplant, two had stable liver disease with mild portal hypertension at ages four and seven, and one died at 13 months.

This is the clearest direct PFIC4 evidence for an early severe course. It fits the mechanism: when TJP2 is absent, the canalicular tight-junction barrier can fail and bile constituents can leak or reflux into liver tissue.

The 2024 Lal et al. cohort strengthens this variant-class signal. In 44 children with TJP2 deficiency-related cholestasis, those with predicted protein-truncating mutations on both alleles, labeled TJP2-C, had more early infantile cholestasis, less jaundice clearance, more ascites, higher serum bile acids, and much lower native-liver survival than children with at least one missense allele. Median age at death or liver transplant in TJP2-C was 2.5 years.

## What is known about milder, recurrent, or later disease

Zhang et al. 2020 reviewed seven pediatric patients with biallelic `TJP2` variants and found a broader spectrum than the original severe cohort. Their patients had remitting and persistent cholestasis; six responded to cholestyramine, ursodeoxycholic acid, or both; none required liver transplant or developed HCC or cholangiocarcinoma during the reported follow-up. Missense variants in that report decreased but did not abolish TJP2 expression in vitro.

Familial hypercholanemia in Amish patients is an older example that `TJP2` biology can cause pruritus and fat malabsorption without classic progressive liver disease. That disorder involved complex inheritance with `TJP2` and `BAAT`, so it should be treated as spectrum context, not direct PFIC4 prognosis.

BRIC-like or recurrent cholestasis has also been reported with a single heterozygous `TJP2` variant of uncertain functional significance. That is weak evidence for causality, but it supports the broader idea that PFIC, BRIC, ICP, and adult cholestasis can overlap across cholestasis genes.

Adult family reports add another caution. Wei et al. 2020 described a homozygous nonsense `TJP2` variant in a large family with very variable expression: some homozygous adults had cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and HCC, while other homozygous siblings had elevated liver enzymes without cirrhosis at the time of evaluation. The authors raised age-dependent penetrance and modifier variants as possible explanations.

## Genotype and phenotype uncertainty

| Variant or evidence pattern | What it suggests | How strong it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Biallelic predicted protein-truncating variants on both alleles | Higher risk of early infantile cholestasis, persistent jaundice, ascites, high serum bile acids, transplant, or death without timely transplant | Strongest PFIC4-specific cohort signal, especially Lal et al. 2024 |
| Variants predicted to abolish translation and absent hepatic TJP2 protein | Severe infantile low-GGT cholestasis and high transplant burden | Strong direct mechanistic and clinical evidence from Sambrotta et al. 2014 |
| Missense variants with reduced but not absent protein expression | Possible residual function and milder/remitting disease in some patients | Moderate evidence from Zhang et al. 2020 and later reports |
| Homozygous missense p.Gly532Arg with variable severity | Same apparent variant can still show pronounced variability | Emerging evidence from a small 2025 family series |
| One missense plus one truncating allele | Intermediate risk is plausible, but not deterministic | Supported by Lal et al. grouping, still small numbers |
| Heterozygous `TJP2` variants in ICP, BRIC-like, or adult cryptogenic cholestasis | May contribute to susceptibility, triggers, or modifier burden | Weaker and more context-dependent; do not overread without genetics review |

The practical interpretation is not "missense means mild" or "loss-of-function means hopeless." It is better to ask whether the exact variants are predicted to remove protein entirely, disrupt splicing, alter a key domain, or leave residual function, then compare that with the actual phenotype: bile acids, bilirubin, GGT, pruritus, growth, vitamins, INR, albumin, platelets, spleen size, imaging, elastography, fibrosis/cirrhosis, AFP, and treatment response.

## HCC and progression

PFIC4 has published childhood and adult HCC reports. Zhou et al. reported pediatric HCC in TJP2 deficiency, including a child with neonatal-onset intermittent jaundice who presented with acute liver failure and multifocal HCC, and another child transplanted at two years with cirrhosis and HCC found in the explant. Wei et al. reported adult cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and liver cancer in a homozygous `TJP2` family.

This does not produce a reliable PFIC4-specific percentage risk. The evidence base is too small and biased toward severe reported cases. It does support asking the care team for a concrete HCC surveillance plan: when to start ultrasound and AFP, whether surveillance is every six months, and whether the threshold differs with fibrosis, cirrhosis, genotype, or transplant evaluation.

## How to use this in appointments

Questions to ask the clinical team:

- What are the exact `TJP2` variants, transcript, cDNA/protein notation, zygosity, and phase?
- Are both alleles predicted protein-truncating mutations, or is one/both missense, splice, in-frame deletion, or uncertain?
- Is there published evidence for these exact variants or nearby variants in the same domain?
- Is there evidence of residual TJP2 function, either from the literature, variant class, RNA/splicing data, protein studies, or liver immunostaining if tissue already exists?
- Does the patient's observed course fit the predicted variant severity, or does it suggest modifier genes, a second diagnosis, or reanalysis?
- What objective markers define improvement, stability, or progression over the next 3-12 months?
- What exact findings would trigger biliary diversion discussion, transplant-center evaluation, or HCC workup?

## Evidence limits

- PFIC4 cohorts remain small, and many papers are case reports or family reports.
- Severe cases are more likely to be published, so case reports can overstate progression risk.
- Milder or intermittent disease may be underdiagnosed if jaundice resolves or if genetic testing is not done.
- Genotype categories are useful but incomplete; variant interpretation can change as ClinVar, functional studies, and new cohorts grow.
- Treatment response can change symptoms and labs, but PFIC4-specific proof that medical therapy prevents fibrosis, HCC, or transplant remains limited.
