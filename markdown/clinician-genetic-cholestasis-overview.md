## Umbrella framing

Genetic cholestasis is best framed as a set of inherited disorders that disrupt bile formation, bile movement, bile-acid chemistry, or bile-duct development. The labels PFIC, BRIC, ICP, Alagille syndrome, and bile acid synthesis defects are useful clinical entry points, but they are not equal categories:

- PFIC is usually a severe, early-onset, progressive phenotype.
- BRIC is usually an episodic phenotype with attacks separated by well intervals.
- ICP is a pregnancy-triggered phenotype that usually resolves after delivery.
- Alagille syndrome is a multisystem developmental disorder of bile-duct paucity and Notch signaling.
- Bile acid synthesis defects are metabolic enzyme or conjugation defects that make too little normal primary bile acid and/or too much atypical toxic bile-acid intermediate.

The same gene can sit under more than one phenotype label. For example, `ATP8B1`, `ABCB11`, `ABCB4`, and `TJP2` can appear in severe pediatric disease, recurrent cholestasis, pregnancy-triggered cholestasis, or adult-onset cholestatic disease depending on genotype, zygosity, modifier burden, and triggers.

## Mechanism-first map

| Mechanism group | Representative genes | Common clinical labels | Practical read |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Canalicular membrane stability and bile-acid/phospholipid transport | `ATP8B1`, `ABCB11`, `ABCB4` | PFIC1, PFIC2, PFIC3, BRIC, ICP, adult cholestatic disease | Classic PFIC/BRIC/ICP overlap. GGT is often low/normal in `ATP8B1` and `ABCB11`, high in `ABCB4`. |
| Tight-junction and epithelial barrier dysfunction | `TJP2`, `USP53`, probably `LSR` in some reports | PFIC4 and newer low-GGT cholestasis phenotypes | Barrier failure can permit paracellular bile reflux/leakage even when bile-acid transport is not the primary pump defect. |
| Nuclear regulation of bile-acid homeostasis | `NR1H4` | PFIC5 / FXR deficiency | Can present as severe neonatal low-GGT cholestasis with coagulopathy, high AFP, and rapid progression. |
| Intracellular trafficking, polarity, and vesicle-fusion defects | `MYO5B`, `KIF12`, `VPS33B`, `VIPAS39` | PFIC6 and newer numbered/overlapping PFIC-like phenotypes | Transporters may be present but mislocalized; extrahepatic disease may dominate in some genes. |
| Basolateral bile-acid export and enterohepatic handling | `SLC51A`, `SLC51B` | Primary bile-acid malabsorption / cholestatic liver disease | Can combine diarrhea, malabsorption, vitamin deficiency, and liver injury. |
| Cholangiocyte, ciliary, or ductal-development disease | `JAG1`, `NOTCH2`, `ZFYVE19`, `KIF12`, `DCDC2`, `CLDN1` | Alagille syndrome, high-GGT cholestasis, neonatal sclerosing cholangitis, ciliopathy-associated cholestasis | High GGT, duct paucity/proliferation, sclerosing/cholangiopathic patterns, or multisystem findings should shift the differential. |
| Bile acid synthesis or conjugation defects | `HSD3B7`, `AKR1D1`, `CYP7B1`, `CYP27A1`, `AMACR`, `BAAT` | Congenital bile acid synthesis defects / conjugation defects | Often low or normal serum primary bile acids with atypical bile acids; treatment logic differs because primary bile-acid replacement can be disease-targeted. |

| `SLC51A` / `SLC51B` | Autosomal recessive | Variable; can be high | Congenital diarrhea, malabsorption, bleeding/vitamin deficiency, poor weight gain, early fibrosis in reported cases | Nutrition, fat-soluble vitamins, diarrhea, and bile-acid handling need integrated management; not simply a canalicular pump disease. |

| Bile acid synthesis/conjugation genes including `HSD3B7`, `AKR1D1`, `CYP7B1`, `CYP27A1`, `AMACR`, `BAAT` | Usually autosomal recessive | Low/normal or high depending on defect; serum primary bile acids may be low/normal | Poor growth, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency, hepatosplenomegaly, neurologic features in selected defects; pruritus can be absent | Urine/serum bile-acid profiling is key; cholic acid, chenodeoxycholic acid in selected defects, or glycocholic-acid strategies may be disease-targeted. |

## PFIC, BRIC, and ICP as a spectrum

PFIC historically described pediatric progressive cholestasis caused by defects in bile secretion or canalicular function. BRIC and ICP are better understood as milder, episodic, or trigger-dependent expressions of overlapping biology rather than entirely separate diseases.

| Phenotype label | Typical pattern | Genes most often discussed | Distinguishing point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PFIC | Infantile/childhood cholestasis, severe pruritus, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency, progression risk | `ATP8B1`, `ABCB11`, `ABCB4`, `TJP2`, `NR1H4`, `MYO5B`, `USP53`, `KIF12`, `ZFYVE19`, others | Severe pediatric end of the spectrum; some subtypes carry HCC/CCA risk. |
| BRIC / recurrent intrahepatic cholestasis | Intermittent attacks of jaundice/pruritus with symptom-free intervals | `ATP8B1`, `ABCB11`, sometimes other PFIC-associated genes | "Benign" can be misleading; some patients progress or sit between BRIC and PFIC. |
| ICP | Pruritus and elevated bile acids in pregnancy, usually resolving postpartum | `ABCB4`, `ABCB11`, `ATP8B1`, `TJP2`, `NR1H4`, `ABCC2`, common/polygenic contributors | A pregnancy stress test for bile-acid transport capacity. Severe, recurrent, or early ICP should prompt genetic/liver-risk discussion. |

Recent ICP guidance keeps bile-acid concentration central: diagnosis is usually pruritus plus elevated non-fasting bile acids after exclusion of other causes, and adverse fetal-risk discussion intensifies at higher bile-acid strata, especially around 40 and 100 umol/L. For this packet, the genetic point is narrower: recurrent, severe, early, familial, or postpartum-persistent ICP should not be dismissed as only pregnancy physiology. It can reveal heterozygous or incompletely penetrant transporter/regulatory risk, especially `ABCB4` and `ABCB11`, with smaller or less certain contributions from `ATP8B1`, `TJP2`, `NR1H4`, and `ABCC2`.

## How Alagille fits

Alagille syndrome belongs in the same genetic cholestasis umbrella, but it is not primarily a canalicular pump disease. It is usually autosomal dominant `JAG1` disease, less often `NOTCH2`, affecting Notch signaling and bile-duct development.

Clinical clues:

- Neonatal or infant cholestasis, often high GGT.
- Bile duct paucity may be absent early and become clearer with age.
- Cardiac, vascular, renal, skeletal, eye, growth, and facial-feature findings can be diagnostically important.
- Liver severity is variable, from improving cholestasis to refractory pruritus, portal hypertension, and transplant.
- IBAT inhibitors are now label-supported for cholestatic pruritus in Alagille syndrome, but multisystem surveillance remains central.

## How bile acid synthesis defects fit

Bile acid synthesis defects can mimic PFIC because they can cause neonatal/infant cholestasis, poor growth, malabsorption, and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency. The key difference is biochemical: the liver is not simply retaining too many normal bile acids. It may be making too little normal primary bile acid and accumulating atypical hepatotoxic intermediates.

Practical clues:

- Serum bile acids may be low, normal, or misleadingly not elevated despite cholestasis.
- Pruritus may be absent or less prominent in classic `HSD3B7` / `AKR1D1` presentations.
- Urine or serum bile-acid profiling by mass spectrometry can be decisive.
- Disease-targeted treatment may involve cholic acid, chenodeoxycholic acid in selected defects, glycocholic acid for conjugation defects, plus fat-soluble vitamins and nutrition.
- IBAT inhibition is not the core treatment logic when the problem is failure to synthesize/conjugate normal primary bile acids.

## GGT and bile-acid pattern as triage clues

| Pattern | More suggestive of | Caveat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low/normal GGT with high serum bile acids and severe pruritus | `ATP8B1`, `ABCB11`, `TJP2`, `NR1H4`, `MYO5B`, `USP53`, `LSR` | GGT is a clue, not a diagnosis. |
| High GGT cholestasis | `ABCB4`, Alagille syndrome, `KIF12`, `ZFYVE19`, `DCDC2`, `CLDN1`, obstructive/cholangiopathic disease | Exclude biliary atresia and acquired obstruction/inflammation when clinically relevant. |
| Cholestasis with low/normal serum primary bile acids or atypical bile acids | Bile acid synthesis/conjugation defects | Requires specialized bile-acid profiling; routine total bile acids can miss the signal. |
| Pregnancy cholestasis, recurrent episodes, drug-triggered cholestasis, gallstones/LPAC, family history | Heterozygous or milder variants in `ABCB4`, `ABCB11`, `ATP8B1`, `TJP2`, others | Adult phenotypes may be multifactorial and incompletely penetrant. |

## Newer genes and nomenclature caution

PFIC numbering is less stable than gene-based naming. Older shorthand of PFIC1-3 maps cleanly to `ATP8B1`, `ABCB11`, and `ABCB4`; PFIC4 to `TJP2`; PFIC5 to `NR1H4`; and PFIC6 is often used for `MYO5B`. Later literature uses additional numbered labels for genes including `USP53`, `KIF12`, `ZFYVE19`, `SEMA7A`, `VPS33B`, and sometimes `SLC51A` or other emerging loci. These assignments are still consolidating across reviews, OMIM entries, and clinical labs.

For clinical work, gene-first language is usually clearer:

- Say "`TJP2`-associated cholestasis / PFIC4" rather than relying only on PFIC4.
- Say "`ABCB4`-associated cholestasis" when discussing PFIC3, ICP, LPAC, stones, fibrosis, or adult disease.
- Say "bile acid synthesis defect due to `HSD3B7`" rather than grouping it under PFIC.
- Document zygosity, variant class, transcript, inheritance, and segregation because the phenotype label alone does not predict severity.

## Diagnostic strategy

Initial workup should still protect time-sensitive diagnoses such as biliary atresia, obstruction, infection, endocrine disease, and metabolic liver failure. Once genetic cholestasis is plausible, the umbrella testing strategy is:

- Use GGT, direct bilirubin, serum bile acids, AST/ALT, INR, albumin, CBC/platelets, growth, stool color, and ultrasound to frame the first pass.
- Order a cholestasis multigene panel or exome/genome approach that includes transporter genes, tight-junction genes, bile-acid synthesis/conjugation genes, Alagille genes, and cholangiopathy/ciliopathy genes.
- Confirm whether deletion/duplication analysis is included.
- Add urine/serum bile-acid profiling when serum bile acids are unexpectedly low/normal, pruritus is absent, or a synthesis defect is plausible.
- Use family segregation testing when biallelic disease or variant phase matters.
- Revisit variants of uncertain significance as phenotype, segregation, new gene literature, and functional evidence evolve.

When using a panel, check what the test actually covers: older PFIC genes alone are not enough for a modern cholestasis differential. The panel or exome/genome workflow should include copy-number analysis where relevant, current PFIC-like genes, Alagille genes, bile acid synthesis/conjugation genes, and genes that point to syndromic intestinal, renal, hearing, vascular, or ciliary disease. If the clinical picture and result disagree, re-review the phenotype and the raw variant evidence rather than forcing the patient into the nearest PFIC number.

## Treatment implications by bucket

| Bucket | Symptom management | Disease-targeted or escalation logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PFIC / transporter-barrier spectrum | IBAT inhibitors, rifampin, bile acid binders in selected cases, naltrexone/sertraline/sedating agents as specialist-directed adjuncts, nutrition/vitamins | Genotype affects IBAT expectations, malignancy risk, biliary diversion candidacy, and transplant timing. |
| Alagille syndrome | IBAT inhibitors for cholestatic pruritus, nutrition/vitamins, xanthoma and sleep management | Multisystem surveillance for cardiac, renal, vascular, skeletal, ophthalmologic, growth, and portal-hypertension complications. |
| Bile acid synthesis/conjugation defects | Vitamins/nutrition; itch therapy usually not the central issue in classic presentations | Primary bile-acid replacement and biochemical monitoring of abnormal metabolites; late diagnosis with advanced fibrosis has worse prognosis. |
| ICP / adult-onset cholestatic disease | Obstetric bile-acid monitoring, UDCA for symptoms in many protocols, delivery timing by obstetric risk guidance | Severe/recurrent/early ICP or family history should trigger hepatology/genetic review and postpartum liver-risk follow-up. |

## Family-facing framing for clinicians

A practical family explanation is: "These conditions all affect the bile system, but at different points. Some genes affect pumps that move bile acids. Some affect the seal between liver cells. Some affect how bile ducts form. Some affect how the body manufactures bile acids. The same gene can cause severe childhood disease in one setting and episodic or pregnancy-triggered disease in another."

That framing helps avoid two common errors:

- Treating all low-GGT cholestasis as the same PFIC subtype.
- Treating a normal or low total bile-acid result as reassuring when a bile acid synthesis defect is possible.

## Unresolved questions for a treating team

- Which exact gene, transcript, variants, zygosity, inheritance phase, and classification explain the patient's disease?
- Does the phenotype fit a transporter/barrier disorder, duct-development disorder, bile acid synthesis defect, or mixed/uncertain category?
- Were deletion/duplication testing and bile-acid profiling performed if needed?
- What malignancy surveillance is appropriate for this gene and fibrosis stage?
- Which response metrics matter most: itch, sleep, serum bile acids, bilirubin, growth, vitamins, INR, fibrosis markers, portal hypertension, or all of these?
- Would a pediatric genetic cholestasis center, adult genetic cholestasis clinic, or research registry add value?
